Throughout various governments, online age assurance has been cited as being the solution to the goal of keeping children protected from harmful content online. This article will critically evaluate online age assurance, looking not only at how it works to keep children safe online, but also whether it poses any additional consequences.

The article draws from guidance from OFCOM, academic research, UK law, and real-world events since the widespread implementation of online age assurance.
While the evidence does suggest that age assurance may support child safety, the methods used to conduct online age assurance remain ethically questionable, with most methods involving personally identifying information, such as legal documents or biometric information.

Overall, online age assurance can be seen as a valid way of keeping children safe online. However, evidence thus far has shown it to be a potential risk factor in the world of cybersecurity and digital privacy.

### Introduction

**[AS OF 05/11/2026] Word Count: 131 (SOURCED FROM MICROSOFT WORD)**

Online platforms are increasingly expected to determine whether users are children or adults, with the expectation being developed by concerns about children's exposure to harmful content online, leading to various laws being implemented in the UK.

"Online age assurance" acts as an umbrella term, generally referring to the methods used to estimate or verify a user's age. However, the process of verifying a user's age to protect children may require users to give up personally identifiable information that can itself create large risks for the user. For example, some methods may range from facial age estimation to scanning government documents through digital ID services. 

This article will examine whether online age assurance should be understood primarily as a way of keeping children safe online or as a threat to user privacy.

### Why online age assurance is being introduced

**[AS OF 05/11/2026] Word Count: 210 (SOURCED FROM MICROSOFT WORD)**

The main argument supporting age assurance is that children may encounter content or services that are unsuitable for their age. Being able to choose your own age without any checks in place has always been a thing children have used to their advantage, which is why governments have started having interests in providing stronger evidence.

The UK introduced and passed the Online Safety Act in October of 2023, with parliament stating that the act would "be fully implemented in 2026" [https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cdp-2025-0043/]. This law places duties on online services to start properly enforcing age requirements, with Ofcom identifying several possible approaches, such as age estimation using a scan of your face or using digital ID services to scan a government-issued ID. These approaches shouldn't be seen as the same thing, however. On one hand, age estimation tries to predict an age, or where a user might belong in a list of age ranges. On the other hand, age verification checks evidence, such as a government document like a passport or driver's license.

The Information Commissioner's Office states that age-assurance systems must consider various concerns regarding privacy, data-protection and user-experience. Their way of introducing age assurance, therefore, reflects a real objective when it comes to safeguarding, while also creating a need to examine how much information is collected to achieve it.

### Privacy and cybersecurity risks

**[AS OF 05/11/2026] Word Count: 319 (SOURCE: MICROSOFT WORD)**

There are numerous amounts of dangers and risks that could be named here, but for now, I'll cover the three main ones most people worry about.

The first main risk is the unnecessary collection of personal information. Typically, online platforms use vendors to provide them with age verification services. While the platforms only receive an age result, the vendor still processes all the personal information, and has the opportunity to use it maliciously, such as storing it for later use. For example, facial age estimation may result in a company storing an image of your face in a database to train their estimation models on it. Another example might be a company storing the details of your government documents, which is to then be sold off for marketing purposes in order to gain a profit off your personal information.

This brings us to the second risk, being a security flaw or failure. A data breach involving documents with personally identifiable information or facial information could have far more serious consequences than a case of simply having a password be revealed, primarily because of how biometric characteristics cannot easily be changed. The existence of breaches involving identity-verification vendors serves as a reason for why data collection should be minimized and why strong security controls matter. Centralised databases may also become attractive targets for attackers.

The third and final risk I'll cover is the loss of anonymity. Users may reasonably want to access information lawfully without platforms learning or tracing their identity. Linking an age check to browsing activity could enable tracking or create opportunities for secondary uses of data. Some concerning examples of "secondary uses" can range from your data being sold for marketing purposes to more severe cases like cyberstalking. Consequently, the question is not simply whether age assurance can identify children, but whether it can do so without creating a new infrastructure for mass surveillance and data exploitation.

### Ethical evaluation: can the risks be justified?

**[AS OF 05/11/2026] Word Count: 258 (SOURCE: MICROSOFT WORD)**

The ethicality of age assurance can be assessed through various factors, such as privacy, autonomy, proportionality, fairness and security. The table below provides a framework for applying these principles.

| Ethical principle | Question for evaluation | Potential concern |
|-|-|-|
| Privacy | Is only necessary information collected? | Identity or biometric data may be collected unnecessarily |
| Autonomy | Can users access services lawfully without excessive monitoring? | Users may lose anonymity or feel pressed to identify themselves |
| Proportionality | Is the measure appropriate to the risk being addressed? | Highly intrusive checks may be used for low-risk content |
| Fairness | Does the system work equally well for different users? | Error rates or access barriers may affect groups unequally |
| Security | Does the system introduce additional risks? | Breaches, identity theft, secondary data use |

These principles suggest that not all age-assurance methods are ethically equivalent.

While a system that requires a platform to receive a full identification document may act as stronger evidence than declaring your own age, it creates far greater privacy risks, especially when it comes to the risk of being in a data breach.

Compared to this, an alternative system could allow a trusted provider to confirm that a user is over a particular age without giving away their identity or exact date of birth, and while it may not remove every risk (because providers must still be trusted and systems must be secure), it better reflects the ethical practices of minimizing data retention. 

With this, we can see that age assurance is most defensible when it's targeted, evaluated, accessible and designed to reveal no more information than necessary.

### Conclusion

**[AS OF XX/11/2026] Word Count: 156 words (SOURCE: MICROSOFT WORD)**

To conclude, this article examined whether online age assurance should be regarded as a means of keeping children safe and protected online or whether it should be seen instead as a privacy threat.

The evidence indicates that it can contribute to child protection by addressing the weakness of sites that let users declare their own ages and supporting platforms' regulatory responsibilities.

Nevertheless, identity and biometric checks can create serious risks involving data breaches, surveillance, inaccurate results and loss of anonymity. The ethical issue is, therefore, not whether age assurance is always acceptable or unacceptable, but rather by which methods are used and under what safeguards.

Age assurance can only be justified when it is proportionate to the risk, technically reliable, fair to different users across a variety of groups, and supported by strong security and the minimizing of data being stored on users. Privacy-preserving age proofs are preferable to systems that routinely expose users' full identities.
